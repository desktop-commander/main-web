The Five-App Trap

Most personal management systems start the same way. You hear about a new productivity app, try it for a week, and slowly migrate some of your workflow. Then another app solves a different problem better. Before long, your "system" is a patchwork:

Tasks live in Todoist or Things. Notes live in Notion or Obsidian. Documents live in Google Drive or your local filesystem. Quick thoughts go into Apple Notes or a random text file. Reference material is bookmarked across three browsers.

Each tool works fine in isolation. The breakdown happens at the seams.

A Getting Things Done forum thread captures this well. One user described the friction of separate systems: when looking at a giant task list, they "had to mentally pause and recall which project it belonged to" — because the context lived in a different app entirely.

Another contributor in the same thread noted that "as the ratio of total notes to embedded actions becomes larger, it often becomes harder for me to manage the project." Tasks without context become meaningless checkboxes. Notes without actions become dead weight.

This is the five-app trap: each tool optimizes for one dimension while creating fragmentation across all the others.

What a Personal Management System Actually Needs

Forget features for a moment. A personal management system that works needs three things:

One place to ask questions. Not one place to store everything — that's impossible and unnecessary. One place where you can ask "where's that tax document?" or "what do I need to do for the client project?" and get an answer, regardless of where the actual files live.

Context that follows you. When you look at a task, you should be able to see the related notes, files, and history without switching apps. When you read a note, the next action should be obvious.

Low maintenance. If your system requires a weekly review, daily inbox processing, and constant gardening to stay useful, it will collapse the first busy week. The best systems work even when you're not actively maintaining them.

Most productivity methodologies nail one of these and ignore the others. GTD gives you context but demands heavy maintenance. PARA organizes files but doesn't help with tasks. Zettelkasten connects ideas but won't remind you to pay your taxes.

Building Your System on Files You Already Have

The most resilient personal management systems are built on plain text. Markdown files, text files, CSVs — formats that any tool can read and that will still work in twenty years.

Here's a practical structure that works well with AI-assisted management:

~/personal/
├── inbox.md          (quick capture — thoughts, links, tasks)
├── tasks/
│   ├── active.md     (current priorities)
│   └── someday.md    (things to revisit)
├── notes/
│   ├── projects/     (one file per project)
│   ├── meetings/     (date-stamped meeting notes)
│   └── reference/    (how-tos, checklists, templates)
├── finances/
│   ├── 2025-taxes/

Where This Approach Has Limits

Being honest about what local AI management can and can't do:

It's not real-time. AI doesn't watch your folders for changes or send notifications. You initiate the interaction. If you need push reminders at specific times, you still want a calendar or task app for that.

It requires some structure. AI can work with messy folders — that's part of the point. But it works better when you establish basic conventions. Consistent file naming and a rough folder hierarchy make a big difference.

It doesn't sync across devices automatically. This is a local-first approach. If you work across multiple machines, you'll want a sync layer (iCloud Drive, Syncthing, or similar) underneath.

Complex recurring workflows need automation tools. For repeating tasks with multiple steps and conditions, dedicated automation platforms are still more appropriate.

The strength is in the space between these limitations: the everyday work of finding, organizing, connecting, and acting on your personal information.

Browser-based AI can't see your local files. You'd need to copy-paste content into the chat, losing all file structure and context. Desktop Commander gives the AI direct filesystem access, so it can search across folders, read multiple files, and make changes — all in one interaction.
